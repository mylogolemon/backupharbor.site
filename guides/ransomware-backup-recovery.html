Backups and ransomware: what should you do first?

Understand why offline copies and careful recovery planning matter after suspected compromise.

By BackupHarbor EditorialPublished 9 November 2026Educational guide

Ransomware can affect both the files you work on and backups that are always connected or reachable with the same credentials. A recovery strategy therefore needs separation, not just a fast copy process. If compromise is suspected, avoid treating a routine restore as the first and only response.

Contain exposure before restoring

If files have suddenly become inaccessible or suspicious software appears, stop normal activity on the affected device. Follow your organization’s incident plan or seek qualified security assistance. Do not connect clean backup drives to a device that might still be compromised, and avoid making unsupported assumptions about the cause.

Find a trusted recovery point

Determine which backup versions precede the incident and whether they are accessible from an isolated environment. CISA recommends offline protected backups and regular restoration testing because attackers may try to encrypt or delete connected copies. Verify the content and integrity of candidate backups before putting systems back into service.

Restore in a clean environment

Recovery may require rebuilding or cleaning affected systems first. For important business environments, professional incident response may be necessary to avoid reinfection and preserve evidence. Once recovered, rotate compromised credentials as advised and improve separation, verification, and retention of backup destinations.

Recognize the limits of ordinary home troubleshooting

When suspicious encryption or account compromise is possible, the immediate priority is to avoid making the situation worse. Restoring files onto a compromised system can expose clean copies, and mass reconnection of drives can increase the damage. This is different from recovering a document deleted by accident. Follow trusted incident-response guidance, isolate affected equipment where appropriate and seek qualified assistance for business-critical or sensitive environments.

Your ability to recover depends partly on whether the chosen backup point predates the incident and remained inaccessible to the attacker. Review logs and backup status carefully, and do not assume a file is clean because it was retrieved from the cloud. CISA’s guidance emphasizes offline copies and restoration tests. After the incident, improve account separation, storage isolation and periodic rehearsals rather than merely adding another always-connected backup drive.

FIELD EXAMPLE

What this looks like in practice

Example: a small organization discovers encrypted documents. Rather than plugging its only external backup into the affected PC, it isolates the device, obtains expert help, and verifies a disconnected backup from before the incident.

Action checklist

Use this as a starting point. Adjust the steps to the sensitivity of your information, the device you use and how you would restore it.

  1. Separate affected devices from clean backups.
  2. Follow trusted incident-response guidance.
  3. Identify a known-good recovery point.
  4. Test restoration before resuming ordinary work.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Do not attach untouched backup drives to a suspected infected machine.
  • Do not treat all cloud-sync history as immune to ransomware.
  • Avoid claims that payment guarantees recovery.

Frequently asked question

Is cloud backup enough against ransomware?

Not automatically. Configuration, version history, access controls, and whether attackers can reach or delete the backup all matter.
