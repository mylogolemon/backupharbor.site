A restore evidence log: what to record when checking a backup

Use a practical evidence table to record the source, restored version, opening test, and next action without exposing private data.

By BackupHarbor EditorialPublished 22 November 2026Educational guide

A backup can report success while the file you need is missing, outdated, unreadable, or dependent on another application. A small evidence log makes the next restore test repeatable and shows what remains unverified.

Choose a representative sample

Select files that exercise different parts of your real workflow: a plain document, a photo or video, and an application project with linked assets if you use one. Use harmless, non-sensitive samples for practice. A single successful photo restore does not establish that a password manager, message history, or entire disk can also be recovered.

Record the category and expected characteristics without copying private content into a shared log. If the actual archive is at immediate risk, preserve the original and consult a qualified specialist before attempting anything destructive.

Record the source and time

Note the backup destination, the reported completion date and time, the time zone when relevant, and the version you expect. “Cloud” by itself is not enough when you use multiple accounts or services. Record a non-sensitive identifier or location description that an authorized person can understand; never put account passwords, recovery phrases, or encryption keys in the log.

A backup completion timestamp is not the same as a restore-test date. Both are useful: one describes how recent the data may be, and the other records when you checked that a sample could be recovered.

Restore to a safe destination and check

  1. Use a fresh, separate folder or disposable device so the existing working copy is not overwritten. Confirm that the chosen backup software supports that destination.
  2. Restore the intended sample and check its name, size, and relevant version. If you expect an older draft, compare a known detail that changed between versions.
  3. Open the restored item in the application you actually need. For a project, check a representative linked asset, font or database where applicable; a successful open of one component is not proof that every dependency is present.
  4. Optionally compare a SHA-256 digest with a trusted reference. A matching digest is strong evidence of matching bytes, but not proof of authenticity, completeness, or a usable application environment.

Use the evidence template

Download the restore evidence template (CSV). Its single row is explicitly an illustrative example. Replace it with your own non-sensitive descriptions before relying on it. Columns record the data category, source, backup completion time, test date, expected version, opening check, result, and next action.

Keep the log wherever authorized people can find it without exposing sensitive inventory details. A CSV file is not encrypted merely because it is a spreadsheet; use the storage and access controls appropriate to its contents.

Classify what happened

Pass: the selected sample was recovered from the intended source and opened with the expected content. Pass with warning: it opened, but a dependency or access issue needs attention. Fail: the sample could not be found, recovered, or opened as expected. These labels describe only the sample tested.

For a warning or failure, write one next action and its owner, such as “check whether the project’s asset folder was included.” Confirm the remedy with another safe test. Do not claim full recoverability until the scope and evidence actually support that conclusion.

Repeat after meaningful changes

Retest after a backup destination, device, account, encryption setting or restore application changes. Choose a cadence that reflects the impact of loss and how often the data changes. Check recent successful runs between tests, and treat an unreachable or untested destination as a gap rather than an established copy.
